How I approach a product

The same four moves shaped both products below. They are the reason a regulated team can say yes to AI.

1

Start with the reviewer's job

Who decides, with what evidence, and what a wrong call costs them. The product is designed around that person, not around the model.

2

Draw the line between machine and human

Define what the system decides on its own and what goes to a person, balancing automation against the cost of a wrong decision.

3

Build trust in as features

Cited evidence, abstain-to-human and an audit trail are product requirements, written as stories with acceptance criteria.

4

Read speed next to quality

Metrics come with guardrails: a fixed evaluation set, groundedness and override rate always sit beside speed and cost.

Case studies

Two regulated-domain products I worked on at Gamaka Intellectual Machines, told as product decisions rather than feature lists.

Medical Insurance Claim Validation Engine

Gamaka Intellectual Machines
Python, LangGraph, Streamlit, ServiceNow
Deployed on GCP: Cloud Run for APIs, GKE for workers

Problem. Manual claim validation was slow, inconsistent and hard to audit. I framed it with medical advisors and claim reviewers, gathered requirements through interviews and written rules, and wrote them as user stories with acceptance criteria in Jira, each linked to its source requirement.

The core product decision

No finding is accepted without a verbatim quote from the source documents. Anything without one returns ABSTAIN to a medical officer, with the evidence attached.

Automation covers the clear cases and people keep control of the risky ones.
Decision flow
What I owned
  • Requirements. Interviews and written rules turned into testable user stories in Jira.
  • Reviewer experience. A Streamlit dashboard with evidence next to each finding, approve, query or deny, and an audit trail of every decision.
  • Delivery. Agile iterations on GCP, with ICD-10 coding by hybrid retrieval feeding six parallel validation agents (LangGraph) with typed outputs (Pydantic v2).
  • Governance. A version-controlled clinical knowledge base, medical-advisor review and bitemporal PostgreSQL history, so any past decision can be reconstructed as the engine saw it on that date.

Agentic AI System for Fraud and AML Risk Triage

Gamaka Intellectual Machines
Python, LangGraph, scikit-learn, NetworkX, FAISS
Deployed as independent containerised services on GKE

Problem. Alert fatigue. Analysts spend most of their time on low-risk alerts, and suspicious activity reports (SARs) must be defensible to regulators. I wrote the requirements and success criteria that the design was built around.

The core product decision

Tiered processing, ML before LLM. Anomaly-detection and graph models pre-screen every alert, so only high-risk alerts reach the LLM pipeline and most never need one.

A ceiling of two LLM calls per escalated alert keeps spend, latency and audit behaviour predictable across both tiers.
Triage flow
What I owned
  • Requirements. The problem framing and success criteria the design was built around.
  • SAR drafting. Grounded by hybrid RAG (FAISS), with every factual claim cited to a source text span and independently verified by a second LLM before an analyst sees it.
  • Backlog. Ten agent specifications broken into epics and stories in Jira, with acceptance criteria for each agent's controls and outputs.
  • Specification. A 21-page spec with four diagrams, controls, provenance and an evaluation plan in Confluence, linked to the Jira work.
